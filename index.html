Push Notifications for a UAE Business App: Opt-In, Arabic and English Messages, Timing and Consent

Push notifications are the one feature almost every business app in the UAE ends up with, and the one most often planned last. A team spends months on screens, payments and the admin panel, then a week before launch someone asks, "What do we send people, and when?" The result is usually a burst of generic promotions, a wave of people switching notifications off, and an app that quietly loses its most direct line to customers.

This guide is for owners and product managers in Dubai and across the Emirates who want to plan notifications and in-app messages properly: what to ask for permission for, how to write in Arabic and English, when to send around the working week, prayer times and Ramadan, and how to stay on the right side of the UAE's personal data law.

Why notifications deserve their own plan

A notification is the only part of your app that reaches a customer when the app is closed. That makes it powerful, and it makes it fragile. On iPhone, the app must ask permission before it can send anything, and a person who taps "Don't Allow" has to dig into Settings to change their mind. On recent versions of Android the same rule now applies. You get one good chance to ask, so the request has to land at the right moment, with a clear reason.

Start with message types, not campaigns

Before anyone writes a single line of copy, list every kind of message the app could send and sort it into three groups. This one exercise answers most later questions about permissions, consent and timing.

1. Service messages

These are messages the customer expects because of something they did: an order confirmed, a delivery on its way, a booking reminder, a password reset, a payment receipt, a reply from support. They are the backbone of a good app and the reason most people accept notifications in the first place. They should be fast, specific and never mixed with promotions.

2. Account and safety messages

Login from a new device, a change of email, a failed payment, a subscription about to renew. These protect the customer and the business. Many of them should also go by email or SMS, because you cannot rely on a push being seen.

3. Marketing messages

Offers, new products, abandoned basket nudges, seasonal campaigns and loyalty reminders. These are the messages people switch off. They need their own opt-in, their own frequency limit and an easy way to say "no thanks" without losing service messages.

Once the list is sorted, write down for each type: what triggers it, who receives it, which channel carries it (push, in-app, email, SMS or WhatsApp), and whether the customer can turn it off. That table becomes part of the app's specification, and it saves weeks of back-and-forth later.

Asking for permission at the right moment

The most common mistake is asking for notification permission on the very first screen, before the customer knows what the app does. Many people tap "Don't Allow" by reflex. A better pattern is the "pre-permission" screen: a short in-app explanation that appears just before the system prompt, at a moment when the benefit is obvious.

  • After a first booking: "Want a reminder the day before your appointment?"
  • After placing an order: "Turn on notifications to know when your delivery leaves the store."
  • After joining a loyalty programme: "We'll let you know when you have points to use."

If the customer says "Not now" on your own screen, you have not used up the system prompt. You can ask again later, at another natural moment. If they say yes, the system prompt follows and the answer is far more likely to be positive.

Keep a simple settings screen inside the app where people can turn each message group on and off. Separate switches for "Orders and bookings" and "Offers and news" are enough for most businesses. When someone turns off offers, keep sending service messages. When they turn off everything, respect it and use email for anything essential.

Consent and the UAE personal data law

The UAE's Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the Protection of Personal Data, usually called the PDPL, sets out how businesses handle personal data, and consent sits at its centre. Device tokens used for push, usage data used to decide who gets which message, and any profile built from purchase history all count as personal data once they are linked to a person. Free zones such as DIFC and ADGM have their own data protection rules, so check which regime applies to your company.

Some practical steps that hold up well in most cases:

  • Separate marketing consent. Do not bundle "send me offers" into the terms and conditions checkbox. Make it a separate, unticked choice.
  • Record consent. Store when and how each customer agreed to marketing messages, and on which version of your wording.
  • Make withdrawal easy. Turning off marketing should take one tap in the app, and the change should reach every system that sends messages, including your CRM and email tool.
  • Collect only what you use. If you do not need precise location to send a store offer, do not ask for it. Location permission is a bigger ask than notification permission and raises more questions.
  • Explain it in plain language, in both languages. Your privacy notice should say what you send, why, and how to stop it, in English and Arabic.

Writing notifications in Arabic and English

In a bilingual market, the language of a notification matters as much as its content. The safest rule is simple: send each message in the language the customer chose in the app, not the language of their phone and not both at once. A push that shows half English, half Arabic is cut off on the lock screen and looks careless.

That means the app must store each customer's language choice on the server, not only on the device, so the system that sends messages knows which version to use. It also means every message template needs two versions, written rather than machine-translated.

Tips for Arabic copy

  • Write the Arabic from scratch for the same purpose, instead of translating the English word for word. A natural Arabic sentence is often shorter or longer than its English twin.
  • Check how the text looks on the lock screen. Right-to-left text with numbers, times, prices in AED or English product names can display in a confusing order. Test real messages on real iPhones and Android phones.
  • Decide on Modern Standard Arabic or a lighter Gulf tone and use it consistently. Formal Arabic suits banks, clinics and government-facing services; retail and food brands often use a warmer style.
  • Keep the most important words at the start. Lock screens cut long text, and the first few words decide whether someone opens the message.

Many of the problems that break Arabic screens break Arabic notifications too: mirrored icons, mixed-direction text, fonts that fall back badly. The Oxedge article Arabic-first UX: 12 things that break is a useful checklist to run through with your designer.

Timing: the working week, prayer times and Ramadan

Good timing is mostly common sense, but it needs to be written into the system so it does not depend on someone remembering.

Respect local time and quiet hours

Most UAE apps serve customers in one time zone, but visitors, expatriates travelling and customers in other GCC countries may not be. Send marketing messages by the customer's local time, and set quiet hours, for example no promotions late at night or very early in the morning. Service messages, such as "your driver is outside", should always go immediately.

The weekend

Since 2022 the UAE government week has run Monday to Friday, with Friday a short day and the weekend on Saturday and Sunday. Many private businesses follow a similar pattern, but not all. Look at your own analytics for when customers open the app and place orders, rather than assuming habits from another market.

Prayer times

Avoiding promotional pushes during prayer times, especially Friday midday prayers, is a small sign of respect that customers notice. Prayer times shift through the year and vary slightly between emirates, so a fixed "no messages from 12 to 1" rule is not accurate. If your messaging tool allows it, block marketing sends in a window around each prayer time based on published daily timings. If not, at least keep Friday midday clear.

Ramadan

Customer behaviour changes completely during Ramadan. Activity often drops during the day, rises around iftar and continues late into the night. A delivery app may see its busiest hour just before sunset; a retail app may see shopping late in the evening. Plan Ramadan messaging as its own season:

  • Move marketing sends to the evening, after iftar, and avoid the hour just before it unless the message is directly useful, such as an order arriving on time.
  • Update any automated reminders, such as "Your basket is waiting", so they do not fire at awkward times.
  • Plan for Eid separately. Messages about delivery cut-off dates and changed opening hours are genuinely helpful.

In-app messages, inbox and WhatsApp hand-off

Push is not the only channel inside an app. Three others are worth planning at the same time.

In-app messages

Banners, cards and pop-ups shown while the customer is using the app. They do not need system permission, so they are a good way to explain a new feature, announce changed opening hours or invite people to switch on notifications. Use them sparingly; a pop-up on every launch is as annoying as a push every hour.

A message inbox

A simple in-app inbox keeps a copy of important messages, such as booking confirmations, offers with expiry dates and receipts. Pushes disappear once swiped away, but the inbox lets customers find them again. It is especially useful for loyalty and membership apps.

Chat and WhatsApp

Many UAE customers would rather talk to a business on WhatsApp than inside an app. A "Chat with us" button that opens WhatsApp with the order number already filled in is often more useful than building a full chat system. If you plan to send order updates by WhatsApp as well, decide which channel carries which message so customers do not receive the same update twice.

Measuring what works

From the first day, track a few numbers for each message type: how many people allowed notifications, how many messages were delivered, how many were opened, and how many people turned notifications off after a message. The last number is the most honest one. If it rises after a campaign, the campaign cost you more than it earned.

A short checklist to brief your developer

  • A list of every message type, grouped into service, account and marketing, with triggers and channels.
  • A pre-permission screen shown at a natural moment, not on first launch.
  • A settings screen with separate switches for service and marketing messages.
  • Separate, recorded marketing consent that can be withdrawn in one tap.
  • Language choice stored on the server, with written Arabic and English templates.
  • Sending by local time, with quiet hours and a prayer-time window for promotions.
  • A Ramadan and Eid messaging plan prepared in advance.
  • An in-app inbox for important messages, and a WhatsApp hand-off where it helps.
  • Analytics on opt-in, opens and opt-outs from day one.

That is the right instinct whoever builds your app: notifications are part of the product, not a marketing add-on.

Frequently asked questions

How many push notifications should a business app send?

There is no single number. Service messages should go whenever something happens to the customer's order or booking. For marketing, start low, perhaps one or two a week, and watch the opt-out rate. If people start switching off, send fewer.

Do I need consent to send push notifications in the UAE?

The phone itself requires permission before any push is shown. For marketing, the UAE PDPL puts weight on clear consent, so ask separately for promotional messages, record it and make it easy to withdraw. Get legal advice on your specific case, especially if you operate in DIFC or ADGM.

Should notifications be in Arabic or English?

Use the language the customer selected in the app, and have each template written in both. Avoid sending mixed-language messages, which display poorly on the lock screen.

Final thought

Customers in the UAE are generous with attention when an app is useful and quick to lose patience when it is noisy. Plan notifications the way you plan screens: around what the customer needs, in their language, at a time that suits their day.
